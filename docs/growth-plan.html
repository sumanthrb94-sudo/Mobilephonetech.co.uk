LeHart Growth Levers

Where a refurbished-phone shop actually compounds — ordered by what returns most per pound, not by what is most fun to build.

The one that matters most

Repeat purchase is not the growth lever here. Trust is.

People buy one phone every two to three years, so you will never win on repeat orders the way a coffee brand does. What you can win is the decision — the moment someone weighs a £499 refurbished iPhone against a £999 new one and decides whether to trust a shop they have never heard of.

Everything below is judged on whether it moves that decision. The trade-in engine and the review engine move it most, and neither is a marketing spend.

Levers, in order

Tagged by when they earn their keep: now pays back inside a month, next needs volume first, later needs a team.

Now

Turn trade-ins into supply, not just a quote form

already built, not wired

You capture trade-in quotes and they go nowhere. That is the single highest-value idle asset in the codebase. Every trade-in is stock you buy below market from someone who has already chosen you — it solves acquisition and supply in one transaction. Wire the quote to a follow-up sequence, a postage label and an inspection step, and your cost of goods drops while your customer list grows.

Now

Collect reviews from real buyers, relentlessly

£0–50/mo

Second-hand buyers check reputation before price. Ask every delivered order for a review 7 days after arrival — long enough to have used the phone, short enough to still care. Genuine reviews only: the DMCC Act bans fakes and incentives-without-disclosure, and I removed an invented 4.8-from-342 rating from your structured data for exactly that reason. Real reviews also unlock rich snippets, which is free search real estate.

Now

Back-in-stock alerts

£0

Refurbished stock is single-unit and sells out. Every out-of-stock page view is currently a lost customer with no way to raise their hand. An email capture on sold-out listings converts unusually well because the intent is already proven — and it tells you what to buy next, which is a merchandising signal you cannot get any other way.

Now

Abandoned basket recovery

£0 on Brevo free tier

People compare refurbished prices for days before committing. A two-email sequence at 4 hours and 24 hours typically recovers a tenth of abandoned baskets, and on a £500 average order that pays for the whole email stack many times over. This is the highest-revenue flow in most refurbished shops.

Next

Per-IMEI inventory and the VAT margin scheme

engineering, then real margin

Not marketing, but it is the biggest margin lever you have. Under the second-hand margin scheme you pay VAT on your margin rather than the full sale price — on a £500 phone bought for £350 that is the difference between roughly £83 and £25 of VAT. It requires a stock book per individual item, which is exactly the per-IMEI model your inventory does not yet have. Talk to an accountant, then build it.

Next

Google Merchant Center and Shopping

free listings + ad spend

Free product listings put your stock in front of people already searching "refurbished iPhone 15". Your structured data now declares refurbished condition, GBP pricing and availability correctly, which is the prerequisite. Start with the free surface, then add paid Shopping once you can measure a margin per SKU.

Next

Content that answers the pre-purchase question

time only

"Is refurbished worth it", "what does Grade A actually mean", "iPhone 14 vs 15 in 2026". These rank, they compound, and they are cheap. Your grading explainer is already the strongest trust asset on the site — build outward from it rather than writing generic blog filler.

Later

Marketplace channels — eBay, Back Market, Amazon Renewed

8–15% commission

Painful margin, but they are where the demand already is, and they launder trust: a first purchase through Back Market can become a direct second purchase. Worth it once your fulfilment and returns can absorb the volume without breaking. Not before.

Later

B2B and refurb-as-a-service

sales effort

Small businesses and schools buy in tens, care less about cosmetics, and churn devices on a cycle. Higher order values, predictable repeat demand, and far fewer emotional returns than consumer sales. This is where refurbished shops that outgrow their founders usually end up.

Email, specifically

You asked what to do about marketing email. The short version: send fewer, better-timed messages, and keep the two kinds apart.

  • Two sending subdomains. mail.lehart.co.uk for receipts, news.lehart.co.uk for marketing. Reputation is tracked per domain — this is what stops a bad campaign taking order confirmations down with it.
  • The flows worth building, in order: abandoned basket → post-purchase care → review request at day 7 → back-in-stock → trade-in follow-up → win-back at 18 months, when a phone starts feeling slow.
  • Your existing list cannot be mailed. The addresses collected before this week have no consent record, timestamp or source. New signups now store all three. Re-permission the old ones with a single opt-in request or drop them — an unprovable list is a liability, not an asset.
  • One-click unsubscribe headers are mandatory, not polite, for bulk senders to Gmail and Yahoo. Keep spam complaints under 0.3% or delivery degrades sharply.
  • Warm the domain up. Blasting a cold sending domain with a full list on day one is the classic way to land in spam permanently.
